# Zhipu Earnings Transcript

Prepared Remarks

Tang Jie

Good evening, investors and analysts. Thank you very much for attending our 2026 interim results briefing. I will first give a brief introduction to Zhipu’s basic situation, our recent thinking, and our future technology roadmap.

Large-model technology has been developing for nearly ten years. Since last year, Zhipu has recognized that large models are shifting from “chatting” to “doing,” and we positioned ourselves for this transition relatively early, with a particular focus on strengthening our models’ coding capabilities. In the middle of last year, we released GLM-4.5, our first model to achieve strong results in coding. Since the beginning of this year, we have successively released GLM-5.0, 5.1, 5.2, and 5.3, all of which have continued to iterate on the same foundation.

First, does scaling necessarily mean substantially increasing the number of parameters? Scaling is a key path toward higher intelligence, but its essence is not simply expanding parameter count. Since the beginning of this year, we have significantly strengthened the scale of the base model, post-training, and task environments. While expanding parameter scale, it is also necessary to increase the model’s effective depth—for example, by using a Loop Transformer to allow the model to think more deeply during inference. We have recently been advancing related work. At the same time, there remains substantial room for improvement in post-training. We therefore invested heavily in GLM-5.3 and significantly strengthened its post-training.

Coding also encompasses many different types of tasks. Strengthening the model across a range of coding tasks can systematically improve its overall capabilities. We did a great deal of work on different task environments, which significantly improved GLM-5.3’s performance and brought it to a SOTA level and the current frontier of intelligence.

On the other hand, a model must not only be powerful; users must also be able to afford and access it. When designing a model, we need to consider model size and active parameters, as well as inference and infrastructure efficiency. Only by reducing inference costs can the technology be made broadly accessible.

For this reason, when designing the GLM-5 series, we placed particular emphasis on strengthening inference capabilities. In a computing environment where Nvidia chips coexist with a variety of domestic Chinese chips, we drew on external resources and acquired an inference-technology company to further strengthen our inference capabilities, making GLM-5.1, 5.2, and 5.3 easier to deploy and use.

Current model usage is substantial, and our usage volume on OpenRouter at one point ranked first. After each new model release, call volume on our MaaS platform has generally doubled. That is a basic overview of our overall positioning and progress this year.

For a model R&D company, the core task remains continuously pushing up the frontier of intelligence. This brings us back to Zhipu’s most fundamental vision. In 2019, we set a single vision for Zhipu: to make machines think like humans. To this day, we are still exploring the upper bound of intelligence. Exploring that upper bound is not only about training models; it also requires us to think about what kind of intelligence future models should possess and what they should be able to accomplish. We have developed several lines of thought around the next stage of our roadmap.

First, at the beginning of this year, we saw that coding had become a direction broadly recognized by the industry. We then thought further about how models could accomplish more tasks on top of their coding capabilities. Around GLM-5.1 and 5.2, we proposed the concept of the Long Horizon Task, hoping that models could execute longer-horizon tasks; we also proposed Autonomous AI, meaning that a model should be able to complete a task autonomously.

Once models possess these capabilities, they will still need to evolve further. We have already begun research into fully self-training models. We hope models will be able to autonomously learn through pre-training, mid-training, and post-training, and autonomously build their own harnesses, so that the training process gradually becomes autonomous.

You may have noticed that in our recent inference optimizations—especially during the release of GLM-5.3 and the optimization of infrastructure for domestic chips—we have already been using an Infra Agent. The Infra Agent powered by GLM-5.3 can autonomously revise optimization plans for domestic chips, significantly improving results. In the future, we will continue along this path and further strengthen these capabilities.

Finally, as we work to make AI more broadly accessible and usable by more people, social responsibility is equally important. The release and large-scale use of models require robust governance and safety mechanisms. We continue to consider how to strengthen models’ capabilities in social governance, public safety, and ethics, and we have already begun related research. We also hope investors and analysts will offer more suggestions and support as we jointly explore the frontier of AGI and the path toward broadly accessible AI.

Liu Debing

Next, I will give a comprehensive report on our achievements and thinking in the first half of the year. In summary, if last year’s keyword was the “intelligence frontier,” then the keyword for the first half of this year was “turning capability into value.” For the first time, models have been proven to be not merely tools, but systems capable of independently completing a task. Our revenue structure changed accordingly, and the commercial value of strong models began to be realized at scale.

The first section concerns our thinking on strategic focus and model development. First, the intelligence frontier determines the task boundary, while task value determines the commercial opportunity. At our annual results briefing in March, we proposed a formula:

AGI commercial value = intelligence frontier × scale of token consumption

The intelligence frontier determines what tasks a model can handle and how much user value each token can create. The higher the intelligence frontier, the higher the value density of the tasks the model can process, and the greater the economic output corresponding to each unit of intelligence. The scale of token consumption reflects the breadth and depth with which intelligence is being used. The same token, when used for casual conversation versus repairing a vulnerability in a production environment, may generate outcomes differing by several orders of magnitude. The value density of the scenario in which a token is used therefore depends on the task boundary the model is able to open up. Each time that task boundary rises by one level, the market opportunity may expand by an order of magnitude.

Our second point concerns the five stages of model-capability development. We believe the evolution of large-model capabilities is not a set of parallel product lines, but a staircase that progresses in sequence:

Chat → Coding → Agent → Cowork → Autonomous AI

Chat delivers a one-off answer. Coding delivers executable code. Agent delivers a completed multi-step chain of tasks. Cowork delivers work products that can be reviewed by professionals.

Autonomous AI delivers the capability to operate continuously. Each step upward has a specific technical threshold. Unless that threshold is crossed, the business model at the next level cannot be established. From Chat to Coding, the threshold is whether the result can be verified. From Coding to Agent, the threshold is long-horizon planning and error recovery. From Agent to Cowork, the threshold is whether reliability is high enough that professionals are willing to review the result rather than rebuild it from scratch. From Cowork to Autonomous AI, the threshold is whether the model can autonomously determine whether its result is correct. During the reporting period, the company’s focus was between Coding and Cowork. We have already begun experiments in cybersecurity, legal services, finance, education, and other fields. Cybersecurity has progressed the fastest; the other directions remain at an early stage and have not yet generated revenue at scale.

Our models achieved a score of 84.5 on CyberGym, surpassing Fable5 and GPT-5.6 SOLO. On ExploitGym, they completed 130 tasks, second only to Fable5 and GPT-5.6.

Our third point is the importance of maintaining a continuously improving model-iteration trajectory and SOTA-level performance. Over the past year, the industry’s understanding of SOTA has been changing. Short-term leadership on a leaderboard is no longer the most important thing. What truly matters is who can continuously bring stronger models to market at a faster pace and at a better cost. A single-point championship is fleeting; sustained excellence is a trajectory that keeps moving upward.

The GLM series completed six iterations in approximately eleven months. Its intelligence index rose from 32 to 60, while cost per task remained around the US$0.20 level. GLM-5.3 Flash entered the intelligence-cost frontier with a cost per task of US$0.045. With coding as its entry point, GLM-5 opened up long-horizon intelligence. Its capabilities expanded from code generation to system understanding, task planning, tool use, and engineering interaction, pushing models from vibe coding toward agentic engineering. Programming has become the company’s main source of revenue, and the long-horizon task capabilities accumulated through coding have also been validated through transfer to fields such as cybersecurity.

GLM-5.2 is a flagship model for long-horizon tasks. It supports a genuinely usable 1M-token context window and, in practice, can accommodate project-level engineering context. It is more stable in long-horizon task execution and more reliable in following engineering conventions. Its success rate in development scenarios has improved further, and it can complete an entire development chain—from requirements to deployable outputs across multiple endpoints—in a single task.

GLM-5.3 is Zhipu’s latest flagship model, with comprehensive advances in complex software engineering and Agent tasks. It uses the same base model as GLM-5.2, and all of its capability gains came from post-training. GLM-5.3 further scaled up its task environments, so that training tasks were no longer limited to conventional programming problems but more closely resembled complete units of professional work performed by experts in real-world settings. With the same architecture, the same total parameter count, and the same number of active parameters, merely expanding the scale of post-training improved end-to-end completion rates by more than 50%. GLM-5.3 demonstrates that scaling is not only about parameters; training is currently the area with the greatest room for improvement.

If GLM-5.3 represents the current capability frontier, then GLM-5.3 Flash represents the cost frontier. Designed for high-frequency, large-scale, cost-sensitive usage, GLM-5.3 Flash uses a new architecture developed specifically to reduce costs. It has 320 billion total parameters and 18 billion active parameters, combines sparse attention with linear attention, and is priced at one-tenth the level of GLM-5.2. Its performance in both benchmarks and real-world applications is superior to GLM-5.2.

Before release, the model was tested under the anonymous model names OX and Aux Offer through real-world usage on OpenCode and OpenRouter. On its first day online, it ranked first on OpenRouter and set a new record for single-day token usage. Total usage over six days exceeded 62 trillion tokens, driving overall platform usage up by more than 20%.

Our fourth point is progress in “using domestic chips to power domestic models.” Since the beginning of the year, we have incorporated domestic Chinese chips into our primary inference capacity. GLM-5.3 Flash is the company’s first model to provide services entirely through domestic-chip clusters under ultra-large-scale, real-world traffic. The chips are connected through our self-developed high-bandwidth interconnect network, and the entire inference engine was substantially accelerated by an Infer Agent powered by GLM-5.3, cutting operator-development cycles in half. For the first time within the company, we have completed the loop in which “the model optimizes the system, and the system runs the model.”

Compared with the initial baseline on the same hardware, end-to-end service performance improved threefold. The company has achieved large-scale, low-cost inference on approximately 100,000 domestic accelerator cards, and inference cost per token has fallen by 80% compared with the beginning of the year. Against a backdrop of persistently tight global compute supply, this path means we have begun to take control of our own cost curve.

First, the revenue mix has undergone a fundamental change. In the first half of 2026, the open-platform and API business accounted for 86.5% of revenue. As model intelligence improved, new scenarios and business formats were unlocked one after another.

As models become capable of independently completing increasingly complete tasks, what customers purchase increasingly resembles the result itself, and the unit in which revenue is measured changes accordingly. Before 2025, in the first stage, the company primarily developed locally deployed-model business. At that time, early models were not yet capable of independently completing a full job. Users were buying a tool, and that tool needed to be integrated, customized, and delivered into the customer’s own environment. From the beginning of 2025, the company gradually entered the second stage: the Coding stage. The defining characteristic of this stage was the model’s transition from knowledge-driven behavior to task-driven behavior. Improvements in programming capability drove rapid growth in APIs and subscription products such as CodingPlan.

At this stage, the company strategically scaled back software-licensing business for locally deployed models and shifted toward continuously providing intelligence services that are callable, scalable, and measurable. Since the beginning of this year, the company has entered the third stage: the Agentic and Cowork stage. The business is moving from model calls toward task delivery. In the first half of 2026, GLM models continued to improve in Agent planning, tool use, and sustained execution of long-horizon tasks. The business also expanded from single-point coding applications into high-value scenarios such as software engineering, cybersecurity, data analysis, and complex automation. Looking ahead, Cowork is precisely the stage the company is now opening up. Models connect to enterprise data, tools, and business systems and are embedded in procurement, finance, customer service, R&D, IT operations, and other processes. They already possess the foundations needed to move from understanding a requirement to executing the task. Real enterprise workflows can, in turn, become environments and data from which models learn. Cybersecurity is currently progressing the fastest. The other areas remain at an early stage and have not yet generated revenue at scale. Connecting the three stages of local deployment, Coding, and Cowork, the core logic is this: each time a model makes a leap in capability and becomes able to complete a more substantial task, what customers purchase moves closer to the final economic value and outcome, and the company’s revenue structure is rewritten accordingly. In addition to model scores on various benchmarks, the industry is paying increasing attention to whether models can achieve a Pareto-optimal combination of intelligence and cost. Relying on clusters of more than 100,000 domestic accelerator cards, the company has initially achieved ultra-large-scale, low-cost inference. In addition to meeting enterprise needs through APIs, we also hope to provide Chinese developers with high-quality subscription-based programming plans. From high-value tasks to the parallel pursuit of frontier intelligence and broad accessibility, and from enterprise services to a combination of APIs and CodingPlan, the company’s go-to-market strategy is becoming deeper, broader, and more balanced.

Liu Debin, Chairman of Zhipu

In the first half of 2026, we demonstrated that a model can independently complete a full job. The next step is to enable a model to undertake a complete business process. We have developed several judgments around this objective.

First, from the perspective of model tasks, models are evolving from long-horizon tasks toward autonomous systems. The current frontier consists of engineering tasks lasting several hours. The next step will extend this to complete interactions lasting several days, enabling a model to maintain goal consistency over longer periods, continuously decompose tasks, call tools, interact with the environment, repair errors, and verify results. At the same time, the industry will move from a single Agent to multi-Agent division of labor and collaboration, with models undertaking complete business processes and moving from assisting work to executing work.

The threshold for this step does not lie entirely in intelligence itself; it lies even more in reliability and auditability. Models need to work without humans checking every step, while still leaving a trail that can be reviewed.

Second, from the perspective of capability evolution, scaling has never stopped and remains the primary path toward higher intelligence. What has not changed is the path; what has changed is the object being scaled. The current upper bound of a model is determined by a combination of four factors: base-model scale, effective depth, training depth, and task environment. The marginal returns of these four factors differ and rotate across stages. The company allocates resources by judging which factor offers the highest current return at each stage.

GLM-5.3 demonstrates that training depth is currently the area with the greatest room for improvement. Once post-training has been pushed to its limit, the next stage of growth must return to the base model. GLM-5.3 Flash has already validated efficiency through a next-generation architecture and a 30 TG multimodal corpus. The next-generation base model is already in training, with directions including greater effective scale, longer native context, and native unified multimodal modeling.

Third, we aim to achieve self-training at the system level. A deeper change is occurring inside the training system: data, environments, and infrastructure are all showing early signs of being taken over by AI.

Data is beginning to generate itself. Model-versus-model self-play pipelines mean that the upper bound of data quality is no longer determined by the speed of human annotation, but by the model’s own ability to verify results.

Environments are beginning to be manufactured. Research agents collect task patterns, judge agents attempt each task, and verifiers and environments are synthesized automatically, gradually becoming standardized products on the pipeline.

Infrastructure is beginning to optimize itself. Inference-system optimization is fundamentally a code-engineering task, and this is precisely the capability domain in which models are strongest.

These three lines point toward the same end state: models participate in their own improvement, forming recursive self-improvement. This is our technology vision—self-training. The next-generation GLM will self-train in environments built by the previous generation of GLM.

Fourth, boundaries and governance. The stronger a capability becomes, the more important it is to place evaluation and judgment in external hands. Assessment of model capabilities and judgments about risk must ultimately be made by people, with independent evaluation by external organizations. In the first half of 2026, we had already begun putting this into practice in cybersecurity. The most sensitive capabilities are released under controlled conditions. Before open-sourcing, independent assessments are conducted by professional third-party teams. Vulnerabilities are responsibly disclosed through the national vulnerability database. Benchmark scores are reported by the company. Risk judgments are left to external teams. This division of labor will not change as capabilities improve; it will only become stronger as those capabilities advance.

Finally, I would like to summarize all of today’s figures in one sentence: the generational level of model capability is the starting point for all of the company’s products and commercial logic. From the GLM pre-training architecture to the Wudao series and today’s GLM-5 series, we have focused on one thing for seven years: training stronger models. Every period of rapid revenue growth occurred after model capabilities improved and new task scenarios were unlocked—not before. Capability comes first; business follows. The reversal in revenue mix and the simultaneous growth in volume and pricing in the first half of 2026 have already validated this logic. Going forward, it will continue to be validated by a larger user base, greater commercial scale, and broader global influence. The road ahead is long, but the direction is firm. Thank you.

Q&A

Q: How do you view Zhipu’s lead in coding capability, and what is the real long-term moat?

A: Any single-point lead has a time limit. Ranking first on a coding benchmark may be matched within weeks or months. More important than any one leaderboard position is the ability to iterate continuously.

Over approximately the past eleven months, the GLM model family progressed through multiple generations—from 4.6 and 4.7 to 5.1, 5.2, and 5.3. Its capability curve continued to rise, while cost per task remained at a relatively healthy level.

The long-term moat is not permanent leadership on every benchmark. It is the ability to maintain faster model iteration, a higher completion rate on real-world tasks, a lower unit cost of intelligence, and continued penetration into higher-value customer workflows.

Q: Why did you choose coding as the key starting point for breakthroughs in model capability?

A: Coding is not viewed merely as a product line that can be commercialized quickly. It is a technical bottleneck on the path toward Agents, long-horizon tasks, complex collaborative work, and Fully Self-Training.

A complex coding Agent must simultaneously understand requirements, break down tasks, read code, call tools, modify code, run tests, re-plan, and verify results. This systematically trains multi-step planning, error recovery, and self-correction.

Whether code runs, whether tests pass, whether performance improves, and whether bugs are fixed can all be objectively verified. Coding therefore provides a scalable, automated, and low-cost feedback environment for reinforcement learning.

GLM-5.3’s training environment has expanded from conventional coding exercises to real engineering tasks. Some of these tasks represent an amount of work equivalent to several consecutive days by a senior engineer.

Q: How can coding capabilities transfer to cybersecurity and other professional fields?

A: Cybersecurity requires understanding code, identifying anomalies, calling tools to verify them, constructing multi-stage attack and defense processes, and adjusting plans based on environmental feedback. Its underlying capabilities are therefore highly aligned with those required by a complex coding Agent.

On CyberGym, GLM-5.2 scored approximately 77.2, while GLM-5.3 improved to 84.5. On ExploitBench, the score increased from approximately 24.4 to 54.4. The closer a task is to a complete exploitation chain, and the more it requires long-horizon planning, the more pronounced the improvement.

Since GLM-5.2, we have worked with multiple domestic security teams on real codebases and identified approximately 2,436 vulnerabilities after expert screening and deduplication. More than 1,000 of these were high-risk vulnerabilities, spanning 269 projects.

The next step is to continue transferring the long-horizon planning, tool-use, and error-recovery capabilities trained through coding into high-value professional work such as data analysis, complex automation, legal services, and finance.

Q: What criteria does Zhipu use when selecting Cowork scenarios?

A: Cowork is not simply everyday office assistance. It means undertaking or replacing real, complex workflows in professional fields.

There are three criteria for selecting a scenario: the task must have a sufficiently high intellectual threshold; the result must be capable of effective verification; and completing the task must create sufficiently high economic value.

Cybersecurity is currently the clearest validated direction. Legal services, finance, data analysis, and other fields are also being explored, but their commercialization timelines will differ because their reliability thresholds and verification mechanisms are different.

If each generation of models can complete longer, more complete, and more professional work, coding will become a meta-ability, and the boundary of high-value tasks the company can serve will continue to expand.

Q: What is the current progress of compute expansion, and what is the structure of your compute resources?

A: Since the beginning of the year, compute capacity has continued to expand at a healthy pace, with increased investment in both training and inference. Sources of compute include self-owned clusters, rented compute capacity, and purchased compute services. Uses cover pre-training, mid-training, post-training, and production inference.

Different chip generations and architectures create a diverse and heterogeneous environment. Training efficiency and inference throughput vary substantially, so a simple count of accelerator cards can no longer accurately reflect compute supply or infrastructure capability.

Internally, we focus more on effective compute: compute that has been installed, can be stably scheduled, can operate continuously over the long term, and can ultimately be converted into model-training progress or billable tokens.

The core measure of a large-model company’s compute capability is not how many cards it owns, but how quickly those resources can support model iteration and how much commercial token supply they can provide.

Q: At what scale are domestic chips being used in Zhipu’s inference business, and how are they performing?

A: As of the first half of the year, we had achieved large-scale, low-cost inference on approximately 100,000 domestic accelerator cards. Our inference supply will not rely on a single channel of either imported or domestic cards as the sole path.

When GLM-5.3 Flash was launched anonymously, the back end used domestic-chip clusters across the board to provide the service. In approximately six days, those clusters handled around 60 trillion tokens of usage and maintained strong service performance under historically high traffic on platforms such as OpenRouter and OpenCode.

The ability of domestic accelerators and inference cards to run the workload has already been validated. The next stage will focus on economics: how to increase the effective-token output per unit of hardware under constraints such as memory capacity, bandwidth, and long-context workloads.

Q: How much has infrastructure optimization improved inference efficiency on domestic chips?

A: The company began executing an “all in Infra” strategy six months ago and rebuilt the inference engine and service stack for GLM-5.3. The optimization covered encode/decode, separation of prefill and decode, quantization, layer splitting, caching, communications, and other areas.

On the same domestic hardware and with the same compute resources, overall end-to-end service performance improved by approximately three times compared with the initial baseline.

Under limited and complex compute supply, improving efficiency through model-infrastructure co-optimization is more fundamental than merely expanding the sources of hardware. The main challenge in the next stage is to convert each unit of physical compute into more intelligence, more effective tokens, and greater commercial value.

Q: What structural issues does training-side compute face, and how will they improve in the future?

A: Training frontier models requires not only a large quantity of compute, but also the stability, network communications, software stack, and long-duration operating capability of large-scale homogeneous clusters.

At present, China still relies mainly on heterogeneous compute. Advanced domestic accelerator cards have not yet fully entered large-scale volume production, creating a certain structural mismatch.

Over roughly the next three to six months, advanced domestic chip manufacturers may begin ramping up volume, and the heterogeneous parallel-computing environment is expected to improve materially.

Training of the base model is already progressing. Compute allocation will prioritize critical training windows, and we will not shift all resources to inference merely because inference demand is growing rapidly.

Q: Does the model layer face a risk of commoditization, and how is Zhipu building pricing power?

A: A single-point SOTA result cannot create long-term pricing power. Performance on public benchmarks and short tasks will gradually converge. However, long-duration, executable, and verifiable real-world tasks amplify the differences among models.

Over approximately the past eleven months, GLM’s Intelligence Index on Artificial Analysis rose from 32 to 60, while the flagship model’s cost per task remained at around US$0.20. GLM-5.3 Flash reduced this further to approximately US$0.045.

Pricing power depends on what a token can accomplish. One million tokens used for casual conversation create very different value for a customer from one million tokens used to complete an engineering project or repair a vulnerability in a production system. As models move from Coding toward Agent and Cowork, customers will gradually shift from buying tokens to buying task outcomes.

Once a model enters a customer’s codebase, toolchain, internal data, and workflows, the customer will accumulate prompts, Agent workflows, permission systems, evaluation standards, and engineering integrations around it. Switching costs will then involve far more than the API price alone.

Q: Have commercial data already demonstrated that capability improvements can support price increases?

A: In the first half of the year, the average API selling price increased by approximately 101%, while token usage also rose substantially. Measured by revenue, the top ten customers’ average daily usage increased by approximately 98 times compared with the beginning of the year.

The fact that prices rose while core customers deepened their usage shows that customers are not using the models only because they are inexpensive. As capabilities improve, customers are willing to entrust more real tasks to the models and pay for stronger capabilities.

The industry may eventually develop two pricing curves. At a given level of intelligence, prices will continue to decline. Frontier models that open new task boundaries and materially improve success rates may still command a premium or even raise prices further.

Q: What stage is the overseas business currently in, and how will it expand?

A: The company has developed overseas business at a certain scale since 2025. Early efforts focused mainly on local deployment and sovereign large-model projects. Malaysia’s Matrix large-model project was one of the earlier overseas initiatives.

As the business format has changed, the overseas focus has shifted toward the open platform and APIs. GLM-5.3 represents higher-level intelligence, while GLM-5.3 Flash represents accessibility and high cost-performance. They occupy distinct ecosystem positions in the high-value-task layer and the broadly accessible layer.

We are actively advancing cooperation with overseas CSP platforms. In the future, we will expand overseas markets through open-source models, local hosting, revenue-sharing, and other approaches. Further progress may be disclosed over the next one to two months.

Chinese models still lag the very top overseas models, but the Pareto-optimal combination of overall capability and cost can become an important entry point for overseas competition.

Q: What are the most important improvement directions for the next generation of GLM models?

A: Scaling has never stopped; the objective remains to raise the intelligence frontier. We previously invested in multimodal, image-generation, and video-generation research, but those directions provided limited help in raising the upper bound of intelligence. We therefore shifted our focus toward text, as well as model forms in which text and multimodal capabilities reinforce one another.

Model capability is moving from answering knowledge questions to completing tasks. The research focus is accordingly shifting from basic question answering toward Coding and Cowork. The next-generation model will continue in this direction.

The base model will continue to expand so that it can better store and represent knowledge. At the same time, we will increase reasoning depth through approaches such as smaller active parameter counts combined with loops, avoiding a situation in which multi-trillion-parameter models become too slow and expensive because their active parameter count is excessively large.

In addition to scaling the base model, post-training will be significantly strengthened. We will use both technical and engineering methods to narrow the gap created by China’s compute constraints relative to overseas companies such as OpenAI and Anthropic.

Q: Why were data environments and post-training important sources of GLM-5.3’s improvement?

A: Around the time ChatGPT was released in 2022, Zhipu had already established a data-annotation team of several hundred people and had been investing over the long term in data and training-environment development.

One important reason GLM-5.3 improved substantially over GLM-5.2 is that the scale of its data environments expanded by several dozen times. We will continue scaling data and environments in the future.

The amount of new text data appearing on the internet is gradually declining, while the share of synthetic data is rising. Therefore, in addition to increasing base-model parameters, it is even more important to build verifiable data environments and real task environments so that models can continue receiving effective feedback during post-training.

Q: What is the Fully Self-Training envisioned for GLM-6.0?

A: Fully Self-Training corresponds to a model with self-evolution capabilities and is also commonly referred to overseas as RSI. The objective is for the model to conduct more of its own training and self-evolution across pre-training, mid-training, and post-training.

The greatest challenge is not simply making the model larger or continuing to train it. It is enabling the model to assess itself, know when to stop, and autonomously correct itself when it makes an error.

Fully Self-Training will become an important direction for future model research. Ethics and social governance will also be incorporated into the model-evolution process.

Q: Why are you currently relatively restrained in parameter scale?

A: The choice of model scale must take into account resources, model objectives, and when users will actually be able to use the model. The amount of training data available domestically is typically around 30–50 trillion tokens. At that data scale, blindly training an especially large model according to scaling laws may not produce sufficiently high marginal returns.

Domestic compute is limited, while the potential of mid-training and post-training has not yet been fully released. Simply increasing parameters is therefore not the most important investment direction at present.

The 745-billion-parameter base model underlying GLM-5.3 completed training at the beginning of the year and was planned from the outset for reuse over more than half a year. On the same base, we successively advanced GLM-5.1, 5.2, and 5.3, along with the corresponding SFT, mid-training, and post-training environment development.

The basic approach is to extract the maximum capability from each base model while optimizing inference costs to a level that users can genuinely afford at scale, rather than merely pursuing a large model that cannot be deployed.

Q: How are you planning the next-generation base model and its inference deployment?

A: The next large base model is already moving forward, although its specific parameter count has not yet been disclosed. Mid-training and post-training will also be systematically planned on top of the new base.

Inference research and engineering have already begun in advance. The goal is for the next-generation model to support full-scale user demand from the first day of release, rather than requiring a long period of engineering adaptation after launch.

An important design constraint for the next generation remains that, once the model is open-sourced, enterprises should be able to install and use it directly, while the company itself should also be able to support large-scale usage through low inference costs.
